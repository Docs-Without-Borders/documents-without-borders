What is The Hague Apostille Convention

The Convention of 5 October 1961 Abolishing the Requirement of Legalisation for Foreign Public Documents simplifies document authentication between member countries through a single Apostille certificate.

The United States has been a member since 15 October 1981. As of 2026, 129 countries are members.

Source: Hague Conference on Private International Law status table.

Understanding Reciprocity

Hague Member to United States

Documents issued in a Hague member country bearing an Apostille are accepted in the United States without additional authentication.

Non-Member to United States

Documents issued in a non-member country require consular legalization through the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and the United States Embassy before acceptance.

United States to Hague Member

Documents issued in the United States bearing a United States Apostille are accepted in Hague member countries without embassy legalization.

United States to Non-Member

Documents issued in the United States for use in a non-member country require United States Authentication and embassy legalization of the destination country.

Hague Member to Non-Member

An Apostille issued by a Hague member is not accepted in a non-member country. Documents require full consular legalization through the issuing country's authorities and the embassy of the destination country, as non-members do not recognize the Apostille system.

United States Position on Inbound Documents

The United States accepts Apostilles from Hague member countries as valid for use without additional United States authentication. This reflects the principle of reciprocity established under the Convention. Documents from non-member countries require authentication and legalization through diplomatic channels.
